# Mastercard: Agentic AI in Payments
## Frozen Fraud-Detection Figures, a Rapidly Expanding Agentic Payments Program, and No Confirmed Performance Metric for Either

## A Note on Scope Before We Begin

This case study covers Mastercard Incorporated's AI and agentic-commerce initiatives across two systems, treated as genuinely separate throughout — not one continuous "Mastercard's AI story":

1. **Decision Intelligence Pro** (announced February 1, 2024) — a generative AI enhancement to Mastercard's existing real-time fraud-scoring service, disclosed with rich pre-launch modelling figures and, as of this case study's writing, no confirmed post-launch performance update in more than two years.
2. **Mastercard Agent Pay** (launched April 29, 2025, under the "Agentic Payments Program"), together with its Acceptance Framework (October 2025) and Verifiable Intent (March 2026, with Google) — Mastercard's infrastructure for AI-agent-initiated payments, disclosed with rich geographic and issuer-rollout detail and, as of this case study's writing, no confirmed transaction volume, resolution rate, or accuracy figure of any kind.

These two systems are disclosed fourteen months apart, address different operational problems (an existing fraud-scoring service under evolving threat, versus an entirely new transaction category with no prior authentication infrastructure), and are never connected to one another in any Mastercard source this case study reviewed. Treating them as one narrative — "agentic commerce created the fraud problem AI now solves," or fusing Decision Intelligence's fraud-scoring pipeline with Agent Pay's authorization flow — is a conflation risk this case study documents and declines to repeat, consistent with this series' central finding at CommBank.

This case study deliberately scopes out several adjacent Mastercard initiatives, mentioned only where directly relevant to context: Mastercard's separate, longer-running Consumer Fraud Risk product for authorized-push-payment fraud; its acquisition of Recorded Future and ongoing Ethoca fraud-signal products; its 2026 Nvidia generative AI model partnership; Mastercard Agent Suite and Agent Connect (business- and merchant-facing tooling built on top of Agent Pay); Agent Pay for Machines (a stated "complementary" machine-to-machine payments service, launched June 2026); and Mastercard's broader stablecoin and digital-asset initiatives. None of these is developed into a full section here. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol and Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth are covered only as necessary competitive and technical context for Mastercard's own Acceptance Framework, not as a full account of Visa's own AI program.

This case study is anchored on Agent Pay for its illustrated workflow and reference implementation (Sections 4 and 4b), because it is the system with a confirmed, sequential set of functions — registration, verification, consumer-configured permissions, human referral, an intent record — that describes an actual process, not just a result. Decision Intelligence Pro's confirmed record is almost entirely outcome figures with no comparable disclosed mechanism to build against.

## Executive Summary

Mastercard's AI disclosures split cleanly into two systems that receive very different treatment from the company itself, and neither treatment is complete. Decision Intelligence Pro launched in February 2024 with three specific, quotable figures — a 20%-to-300% fraud-detection improvement range from initial modelling, a claimed 85%-plus reduction in false positives from Mastercard's own analysis, and a stated intent to scan "an unprecedented one trillion data points." All three are explicitly pre-availability, launch-time claims. More than two years later, no Mastercard disclosure this case study reviewed — not a subsequent earnings call, not a 10-K, not an investor presentation — restates, updates, or supersedes any of them with an actual measured result.

Agent Pay, launched fourteen months after DI Pro under a materially different problem statement (an entirely new transaction category needing its own authentication infrastructure, not an existing fraud-scoring service under pressure), shows the inverse pattern. Mastercard has been unusually forthcoming about where the program is rolling out — U.S. issuers by the 2025 holiday season, global issuer enablement targeted for early 2026, expansions into Latin America, the Caribbean, and Australia, a growing family of related products (the Acceptance Framework, Verifiable Intent, Agent Suite, Agent Pay for Machines, Agent Connect) — and has disclosed no transaction volume, resolution rate, escalation rate, or accuracy figure for the core program at any stage. CEO Michael Miebach's own characterizations remain qualitative and consistent across two earnings calls eight months apart: "it's early days, but we are ready" in January 2026, "early days, but engagement across the globe is energizing" in July 2026.

This case study treats that asymmetry — rich reach disclosure, zero performance disclosure — as its central finding for Agent Pay, the same shape of finding this series has now documented at Lloyds for its customer-facing financial assistant and, in a more extreme form, at Zurich for Clara. A second, genuine ambiguity runs alongside it: a secondary summary of Mastercard's own Q1 2026 earnings call describes Verifiable Intent as "launched," in the same period the FIDO Alliance's own materials describe the underlying multi-network standard as still under collaborative development — a tension this case study states rather than resolves, since no primary source settles which reading is correct. A third finding concerns framing rather than disclosure: Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol and Mastercard's Agent Pay Acceptance Framework, announced the same day and built on the same underlying Cloudflare technology, are easily misread as competing standards when Cloudflare's own account describes parallel, network-specific implementations of a shared authentication layer.

Mastercard's own real financial results for the period this case study covers — 16% net revenue growth to $32.8 billion in FY2025, gross dollar volume of $10.6 trillion, value-added services and solutions revenue growing nearly twice as fast as the core payment network — are attributed by Mastercard to tokenization, security solutions, and broad-based customer growth, not to any single AI product named in this entry, and this case study does not construct a connection Mastercard has not drawn.

The sections that follow lay out Mastercard's firm context and strategic rationale, the two distinct operational problems behind DI Pro and Agent Pay, what is and is not confirmed about each system, an illustrated workflow and a tested reference implementation for Agent Pay specifically, the documented results kept separate by system, this entry's limitations and unresolved tensions, and a forward-looking editorial section — each attributed to its actual source.

## 1. Firm Context and Strategic Rationale

Mastercard Incorporated describes itself, in its own words, as "a technology company in the global payments industry," and its FY2025 Form 10-K states directly: "Mastercard is not a financial institution." *[Source: Mastercard FY2025 Form 10-K, filed with the SEC, February 2026]* That distinction matters for how this entry should be read against earlier ones in this series: unlike CommBank, HSBC, Zurich, or Lloyds, Mastercard does not issue cards, extend credit, or hold customer deposits — it operates the network, brand, and, increasingly, the AI-driven decisioning layer that sits between the banks, merchants, and cardholders who do those things. Mastercard is listed on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE: MA) and headquartered in Purchase, New York.

For fiscal year 2025 (ended December 31, 2025), Mastercard reported total net revenue of $32,791 million, up 16% year over year, and net income of approximately $15.0 billion. Net revenue splits across two disclosed lines: payment network revenue of $19,476 million (up 12%) and value-added services and solutions revenue of $13,315 million (up 23%) — the faster-growing of the two, and the line under which Mastercard's AI-driven fraud and identity products are generally grouped. *[Source: Mastercard FY2025 Form 10-K]* Mastercard does not report segment results below this two-line breakdown; it manages as a single global business rather than through discrete geographic or product segments for financial-reporting purposes.

At the network level, Mastercard's own Q4/FY2025 earnings release confirms full-year 2025 gross dollar volume grew 9% on a local-currency basis to $10.6 trillion, cross-border volume grew 15% on a local-currency basis, and switched transactions grew 10% to 175.5 billion. *[Source: Mastercard Fourth Quarter and Full Year 2025 Financial Results, January 29, 2026, filed as an 8-K exhibit]* By comparison, Visa's fiscal-year-2025 (ended June 30, 2025) payments volume was roughly $14.2 trillion across 257.5 billion processed transactions — a figure this case study has only from secondary financial aggregators, not independently confirmed against Visa's own primary results, and states as such. The two companies report on different fiscal calendars and by somewhat different volume definitions, so this comparison is approximate scale context, not a precise head-to-head figure; both operate as card networks under a "four-party model" and do not themselves issue cards or extend credit.

**Leadership.** Michael Miebach has served as Mastercard's Chief Executive Officer since January 2021, succeeding Ajay Banga (who later became President of the World Bank Group in 2023). As of Mastercard's most recent proxy filings, Greg Ulrich holds the title of Chief AI and Data Officer and sits on Mastercard's Management Committee, leading the company's "approach for leveraging and integrating AI and data across the business, commercializing both internal and external applications and overseeing enterprise-wide governance of these functions." *[Source: Mastercard executive-bio page; Mastercard DEF 14A / PRE 14A proxy filings, 2025]* Ulrich was named to this newly created role as part of an April 2024 leadership realignment that also elevated Craig Vosburg to Chief Services Officer — a role overseeing cyber and intelligence, data and services, and open banking, and explicitly put in charge of Mastercard's "newly formed data and AI organization." *[Source: Mastercard press release, "Positioning Mastercard for the next era of growth," April 2024, reported via Payments Dive, April 11, 2024]* In February 2025, Mastercard named Janet George Executive Vice President of Artificial Intelligence, reporting to Ulrich. *[Source: Mastercard Newsroom, February 19, 2025]*

That same April 2024 realignment is also the point at which Ajay Bhalla — the "president of cyber and intelligence" quoted throughout this case study's Decision Intelligence Pro discussion — retired from Mastercard after a 30-year tenure. *[Source: Payments Dive, April 11, 2024, reporting Mastercard's own press release]* This case study flags that timing directly: Bhalla's on-the-record February 2024 comments describing DI Pro were made roughly two months before his retirement was announced, and he does not appear as a Mastercard spokesperson in any source reviewed after mid-2024. Ajay Banga (Mastercard's former CEO, later World Bank President) and Ajay Bhalla (the retired cyber-and-intelligence executive) are two separate people and should not be confused in any reading of this entry's sourcing.

On the finance side, Sachin Mehra served as Chief Financial Officer through mid-2026; effective August 3, 2026, Mehra moved into a newly created Chief Business Officer role, and Ling Hai — previously President, Asia Pacific, Europe, Middle East and Africa — succeeded him as CFO. *[Source: Mastercard Form 8-K / DEFA14A, filed June 2, 2026]*

**Strategic rationale for AI investment, in Mastercard's own words.** Mastercard's public framing of agentic commerce specifically — as distinct from AI or fraud detection generally — is recent, and, by the company's own description, still an early-stage bet rather than a mature, quantified line of business. On Mastercard's Q4 2025 earnings call (January 29, 2026), Miebach described agentic commerce as "another avenue to enable payment choice with the same trust that we always deliver," adding directly: "It's early days, but we are ready." *[Source: Mastercard Q4 2025 earnings call transcript, January 29, 2026]* On the Q2 2026 call, discussing continued Agent Pay rollout, Miebach used similar language, describing engagement as "early days" but "energizing" across global markets. *[Source: Mastercard Q2 2026 earnings call transcript, July 30, 2026]* This case study treats both remarks as genuine, on-the-record characterizations of a self-described early-stage initiative — not as evidence of scale or adoption, which Section 5 addresses directly and which no Mastercard source quantifies.

Beyond agentic commerce specifically, Mastercard's Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 earnings materials describe "strategic investments... in tokenization, AI capability, and agent-enabled commerce to support the scaling of services and ecosystem advances," and the Q1 2026 call additionally confirmed Mastercard had "launched proprietary generative AI models with NVIDIA" and expanded cyber capability via its Recorded Future acquisition. *[Source: Mastercard Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 earnings call materials]* No Mastercard source reviewed for this case study discloses a company-wide, dollar-denominated AI investment or return figure comparable to Lloyds' "£50 million GenAI value" or HSBC's productivity metrics; Mastercard's AI disclosures are function-specific (Decision Intelligence Pro, Agent Pay) rather than aggregated into one enterprise-level figure, and this case study does not construct one.

## 2. The Operational Problem

Mastercard's own materials describe two separate operational problems, addressed roughly fourteen months apart, not one continuous narrative.

**The first problem: fraud detection racing against fraud technique.** Mastercard's February 1, 2024 announcement opens with the problem framed in the company's own words: "As fraudsters increasingly exploit technology to try to thwart banks and their customers, Mastercard is adopting generative AI techniques to enhance the protections that keep consumers — and the entire payments network — safe." *[Source: Mastercard Newsroom, February 1, 2024]* The same release states that Decision Intelligence — the existing, pre-generative-AI real-time scoring service — "already helps banks score and safely approve 143 billion transactions a year," and frames the generative AI enhancement (DI Pro) as building on that existing capability rather than replacing a broken one. Bhalla's quote in the release characterizes the goal as "transforming the speed and accuracy of our anti-fraud solutions, deflecting the efforts of criminals," and separately frames a second, related goal: reducing false positives — "legitimate transactions which are incorrectly flagged as fraudulent." (One phrase worth flagging directly: no Mastercard language in this release describes existing detection as "rigid" or "rules-based." That framing is a reasonable inference about why a generative AI enhancement would be useful against evolving fraud technique, but it is this case study's own inference, not a Mastercard characterization, and is labeled as such rather than presented as something Mastercard said.)

**The second problem: an entirely new transaction category with no existing authentication infrastructure.** Fourteen months later, Mastercard's framing shifts from an existing service under pressure to a category that, by its own account, didn't previously exist in a form its network could authenticate. The April 29, 2025 Agent Pay announcement frames the problem as consumer behavior migrating toward AI agents faster than payment infrastructure could follow: Mastercard Chief Product Officer Jorn Lambert later described this directly, in a Fortune interview, as a "paradigm shift in commerce" — consumers moving from typing queries into search engines to typing them into AI programs, with the natural next step being "ask[ing] the AI agent to buy it on your behalf." *[Source: Fortune, May 11, 2025, quoting Jorn Lambert]* Mastercard's own April 2025 release frames Agent Pay's job as making sure that when an agent does make that purchase, the transaction can be "securely register[ed] and authenticat[ed]" — i.e., the problem is not fraud in the DI Pro sense, but the absence of any mechanism for a network to distinguish a legitimate AI agent transacting on a real cardholder's behalf from an unauthorized or malicious one. *[Source: Mastercard Newsroom, April 29, 2025]*

**These are genuinely two different problems, not one narrative split across two releases.** DI Pro responds to an existing, high-volume scored process (143 billion transactions/year) getting harder to score accurately as fraud technique evolves. Agent Pay responds to a new category of transaction — one initiated by software acting on a consumer's behalf rather than the consumer directly — that has no precedent in Mastercard's existing scoring or authentication model. Nothing in either release, or in any later Mastercard material reviewed for this case study, states that agentic-commerce transactions caused the fraud problem DI Pro addresses, or that DI Pro was built in anticipation of agentic commerce. Treating the two as one continuous "AI created the problem AI now solves" story would be a fusion this case study's sourcing does not support, in the same sense this series flagged at CommBank for a disputes tool and a voice bot reported as one system.

**What this section does not claim.** It does not claim that agentic transactions "rendered detection obsolete," or that the fraud DI Pro addresses involves "organized fraud rings" — neither phrase appears in either Mastercard release, and this case study does not adopt language stronger than what Mastercard itself states. It does not claim a specific volume of AI-agent-initiated transactions Mastercard was seeing, or anticipated, at either announcement — no such figure is disclosed at launch for either product; Section 5 addresses what, if anything, has since been disclosed. And it does not claim DI Pro and Agent Pay share a technical foundation beyond both sitting within Mastercard's broader "value-added services and solutions" revenue line and both drawing on the company's existing tokenization and network-scoring infrastructure — a structural adjacency, not a stated architectural link.

## 3. The AI Systems: Decision Intelligence Pro, Agent Pay, and Verifiable Intent

### 3.1 What's Confirmed — Decision Intelligence Pro

Mastercard's February 1, 2024 announcement introduces Decision Intelligence Pro as a generative AI enhancement layered onto Decision Intelligence, an already-operating real-time scoring service that, per Mastercard's own description, "already helps banks score and safely approve 143 billion transactions a year." DI Pro's stated function is different in kind from a simple speed upgrade: it "works by assessing the relationships between multiple entities surrounding a transaction to determine its risk," and the release states the new generative AI technology "will scan an unprecedented one trillion data points to predict whether a transaction is likely to be genuine or not." *[Source: Mastercard Newsroom, February 1, 2024]* As established below, the trillion-data-point figure and the sub-50-millisecond scoring claim are both stated in future/forward-looking terms — DI Pro was said to be "available from later this year" at announcement — so neither should be read as confirmed, current performance.

Then-president of Cyber and Intelligence Ajay Bhalla is quoted twice in the release, making two separable claims: first, that the technology is "transforming the speed and accuracy of our anti-fraud solutions"; second, and separately, that "the precision of the solution... has been shown in our own analysis" to reduce false positives by more than 85%. A third figure — initial modelling showing average fraud-detection improvements of 20%, up to 300% in some instances — is presented as modelling distinct from Bhalla's "own analysis" claim. All three are 2024, pre-availability figures; no source reviewed for this case study reports updated, post-launch DI Pro performance data, and this case study does not treat the 2024 figures as current. (Bhalla himself retired from Mastercard roughly two months after this announcement, per Section 1; this case study treats his quotes as accurate representations of what Mastercard stated at the time, not as an ongoing spokesperson relationship.)

One sourcing inconsistency is worth flagging here and returned to in Section 6: secondary coverage (CNBC) describes the underlying model as a "proprietary recurrent neural network," in the same article in which Bhalla is separately quoted describing the technology as using "transformer models." Mastercard's own press release uses neither term, describing the technology only as "generative AI." This case study does not adopt either architectural description as confirmed.

### 3.2 What's Confirmed — Agent Pay, Agentic Tokens, and the Acceptance Framework

Mastercard Agent Pay launched April 29, 2025 under the "Agentic Payments Program," introducing Mastercard Agentic Tokens as an extension of tokenization capabilities the company states already power "mobile contactless payments, secure card-on-file, and Mastercard Payment Passkeys." *[Source: Mastercard Newsroom / Business Wire, April 29, 2025]* The same announcement named Microsoft as a launch partner, integrating Azure OpenAI Service and Microsoft Copilot Studio; a September 10, 2025 follow-up release added Stripe, Google, and Ant International's Antom as collaborators and set a target of enabling all U.S. Mastercard cardholders "before the holiday season," with Citi and U.S. Bank cardholders first. *[Source: Mastercard Newsroom, September 10, 2025]* On Mastercard's Q4 2025 earnings call, Miebach stated U.S. issuers had been enabled and that Mastercard was "working to enable our global issuer base by the end of the first quarter" of 2026. *[Source: Mastercard Q4 2025 earnings call transcript, January 29, 2026]* Regional rollouts followed through 2025–2026, including Latin America and the Caribbean (announced December 2025) and Australia's "first authenticated agentic transactions" (January 28, 2026).

The mechanism Mastercard discloses for distinguishing legitimate AI agents from malicious ones is the Agent Pay Acceptance Framework, introduced October 14, 2025: agents "must be registered and verified before they can transact." *[Source: Mastercard, "Agentic token framework: Driving trusted AI transactions," Oct 14, 2025]* This framework "incorporates and extends" Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth, built on the published RFC 9421 (HTTP Message Signatures) standard. At launch, Web Bot Auth itself existed only as individual IETF Internet-Drafts, not an adopted standard; the IETF's webbotauth working group adopted a formal working-group draft in September 2026, which as of this writing carries no confirmed RFC status. Visa introduced a separately-branded "Trusted Agent Protocol," also with Cloudflare, on the same day (October 14, 2025) — a distinct Visa initiative, not a joint effort. Cloudflare's own account describes partnering with both networks in parallel, with each network hosting its own registered-agent directory on the shared Web Bot Auth layer. This case study does not describe the two networks' approaches as competing standards; Section 6 returns to this distinction in more detail, since it is a genuine point of confusion in some secondary coverage.

On consumer control: Mastercard's August 2026 Signals materials describe Agentic Tokens as configurable — restrictable "by agent, merchant, category, spending limit, timeframe or usage rules." *[Source: Mastercard Signals report / EEMEA newsroom release, August 2026]* No Mastercard source reviewed states what the default configuration is for a consumer who does not actively set these restrictions, or whether Agent Pay requires affirmative consumer configuration before any agent can transact at all; this case study states that gap directly rather than assuming a default.

### 3.3 Verifiable Intent — Announced, Only Partly Confirmed Deployed

Mastercard introduced Verifiable Intent on March 5, 2026, co-developed with Google, to create what the company describes as a tamper-resistant, cryptographically provable record of what a consumer authorized when an AI agent acts on their behalf. *[Source: Mastercard, "How Verifiable Intent builds trust in agentic AI commerce," March 5, 2026]* At announcement, only a specification and reference implementation were open-sourced; Mastercard stated integration into Agent Pay's intent APIs would follow "in the coming months." On April 28, 2026, the FIDO Alliance announced it would develop standards for trusted AI agent interactions, incorporating Mastercard's Verifiable Intent contribution into review by FIDO's Payments Technical Working Group — described as under collaborative development, not a finalized or deployed standard. *[Source: FIDO Alliance, April 28, 2026]*

One tension in Mastercard's own record deserves stating plainly rather than resolving in either direction. A Motley Fool summary of Mastercard's Q1 2026 earnings call (April 30, 2026) describes, among that quarter's highlighted developments: "Launched verifiable intent for agent-initiated payments." *[Source: Mastercard Q1 2026 earnings call, as summarized by Motley Fool]* That summary language is not confirmed as Mastercard's own verbatim wording from the call — it is a secondary characterization of what was discussed, not a direct quote this case study has independently verified against the full transcript. Taken at face value, it is still "launched" language, from the same period FIDO's own materials describe the underlying standard as still under collaborative review. This case study does not adjudicate whether Mastercard's own implementation is in production while the broader multi-network standard remains under development, or whether the secondary summary's "launched" refers to availability of the specification and reference implementation rather than production deployment in Agent Pay itself — no primary source reviewed resolves this distinction, and this case study states the ambiguity rather than picking a side.

### 3.4 Human-in-the-Loop: What Mastercard's Own Data Shows

Mastercard's August 2026 Signals report found 74% of consumers "open to letting [an agent] complete specific commerce tasks at their request." The same release states that "only one in ten" consumers are willing to let an agent complete a purchase autonomously, citing a Gartner press release (May 27, 2026); Gartner's own release attributes the underlying figure to a survey of 322 U.S. consumers (January 2026), describing willingness to let AI make purchase decisions as "topped out at 11%" specifically in lower-stakes categories such as personal care and household supplies — a U.S.-only figure and a category maximum, not a general-population estimate, and notably not Mastercard's own fielded research. Final-approval friction remains, on this evidence, a live design constraint rather than a solved handoff: consumers report broad openness to agent-assisted shopping and narrow willingness to let an agent complete the purchase step unsupervised.

## 4. Illustrated Workflow: An AI Shopping Agent Attempts a Purchase

> **IMPORTANT: This workflow is an illustrative scenario constructed for demonstration purposes.**
> The confirmed functions this illustration draws from — agents must be registered and verified before they can transact (Section 3.2); Agentic Tokens are configurable, restrictable by agent, merchant, category, spending limit, timeframe, or usage rule (Section 3.2); Verifiable Intent creates a cryptographically verifiable record of what a consumer authorized (Section 3.3) — are sourced to Mastercard's October 2025 Agentic token framework page and its August 2026 Signals materials. Everything else below — the named consumer, the specific scenario, the sequencing between steps, and every escalation reason — is this case study's own construction, no richer than the confirmed language supports.
>
> **This workflow does not invent a default spending limit, a default permission set, or a specific confidence mechanism governing whether a transaction falls inside or outside an agent's authority.** Mastercard confirms that consumers set limits and that tokens can be restricted along several dimensions; it does not disclose what happens by default if a consumer has not actively configured any restriction, or what threshold, if any, exists for Mastercard's own network layer as opposed to the consumer's own settings.
>
> **The step sequence below has been fully reconciled against the finished reference implementation (Section 4b), including two additions the build surfaced after this section was first drafted.** An earlier version of this workflow went directly from "the agent attempts a transaction" to a registration check, and separately assumed that a registered, verified agent could be trusted to be acting for whichever consumer a caller claimed. Building and reviewing the reference implementation surfaced two gaps in that assumption — one that let malformed input crash or silently mis-resolve, and a more serious one that let an agent registered to one consumer complete a transaction while claiming to act for a different, unrelated consumer entirely. Both are closed below, consistent with this series' practice of updating the narrative to match what a build surfaces rather than letting the two quietly drift apart. A later, third review surfaced one more, also reflected below: a configured category written differently could be mistaken for one Devon never configured (Step 5).

### The Scenario

A Mastercard cardholder, referred to here as "Devon," has connected an AI shopping agent to their account through Agent Pay. Devon has told the agent it may reorder household staples on their behalf and, separately, has asked it to find and buy a specific pair of running shoes once a size and color become available at a retailer Devon likes.

### Phase 1: Validation, Registration, and Intent

**Step 1 — Agent Attempts a Transaction.** Devon's agent, having found the running shoes in stock, attempts to complete the purchase using Devon's Mastercard credential.

**Step 2 — Input Validity Check.** Before anything else happens, the system confirms the attempted transaction is actually usable: that the agent, consumer, category, and merchant fields are real, non-empty identifiers, that the claimed amount is a real, finite number that isn't negative, and that the transaction date is an actual date. *(CONSTRUCTED: this step, and the strict validation it performs, did not exist in this case study's first draft of this workflow. It was added because building the reference implementation surfaced two ways malformed input could reach later steps — one that crashed the pipeline outright, one that silently produced a false "approved" result.)* A transaction that fails this check is rejected outright — before the registration check ever runs, and before Devon is asked anything — because a negative or nonsensical amount isn't a real purchase attempt to evaluate in the first place. Two distinct reasons are kept separate: the amount or identifying fields being unusable at all, versus a date that is present but not a real date. The second is deliberately not folded into the first — a date the system can't read is a different claim than "the rest of this transaction is nonsense."

**Step 3 — Registration and Verification Check.** The system checks whether the agent presenting the transaction is a registered, verified agent on the network. *(Confirmed as a function; the specific technical check — a Web Bot Auth signature validation, per Section 3.2 — is this case study's own reasonable inference from Mastercard's stated integration, not a step-by-step disclosure.)* An unregistered or unverifiable agent is rejected outright at this point — not escalated to Devon for a decision, since Mastercard's own language describes registration as a precondition for transacting at all, not a condition Devon can override case by case.

**Step 4 — Agent-Consumer Ownership Check.** A registered, verified agent is not, by itself, proof that this agent is acting for *Devon* specifically. The system confirms the agent's actual registered owner matches the consumer the transaction claims to be acting for. *(CONSTRUCTED: Mastercard's language does not spell out this check in these terms, but it is a baseline expectation for any system that lets an agent act "on behalf of" a specific consumer — its absence would mean any registered, verified agent could attempt a transaction under any consumer's name, with nothing downstream positioned to catch the mismatch, since an unrelated consumer's account simply looks like one with no permissions configured yet.)* A mismatch is rejected outright, at this stage, before any permission is checked — this is a fact about who the network says an agent belongs to, not a judgment call about what the agent is allowed to buy.

**Step 5 — Permission and Limit Check.** For a registered agent that is confirmed to belong to Devon, the system checks the transaction against whatever permissions and limits Devon has configured for this agent — merchant, category, spending limit, timeframe, or usage rule, per Mastercard's own confirmed list. *(CONSTRUCTED: Mastercard does not disclose what happens if Devon configured no limits at all for this agent; this illustration does not assume a default — silent-allow, silent-block, or some other behavior — because no source states one.)* If the transaction falls within whatever Devon configured, it proceeds. If it falls outside a spending limit or timeframe Devon actively set, it escalates to Devon — there is nothing to decide, only something to compare. If Devon set no permission for this category at all, it goes to the Authorization Gate (Step 6) rather than proceeding on an unconfirmed default. *(CONSTRUCTED: the reference implementation checks category, spending limit and timeframe, and compares category labels in one canonical spelling so that a configured category written differently, such as "Household_Staples", is not mistaken for one Devon never configured — a fix added after a third review of the build. A different label for the same kind of purchase is still treated as unconfigured; see Section 4b.)*

### Phase 2: Authorization and Record

**Step 6 — Authorization Gate.** This is the point where this illustration diverges most sharply from what a fuller record might otherwise support. Mastercard confirms that agents transact within consumer-set permissions and limits — a category — but does not disclose a specific mechanism, confidence score, or fallback rule for a transaction that is ambiguous relative to those settings (for instance, a category Devon never explicitly restricted one way or the other). This workflow does not invent a rule to resolve that ambiguity. A transaction that has cleared registration, ownership, and permission checks but sits in genuinely undefined territory relative to Devon's configured permissions is treated here as requiring an external decision, not resolved by an assumed default.

**Step 7 — Verifiable Intent Record.** For a transaction that proceeds — whether resolved automatically within limits or after Devon's explicit confirmation — a Verifiable Intent record is created, capturing what was authorized. *(Confirmed as a function per Section 3.3; whether this step is fully integrated into Agent Pay's production intent APIs, as opposed to available only via specification and reference implementation, is the unresolved tension Section 3.3 states directly and does not resolve.)*

**Step 8a — Transaction Completes.** If the transaction is within Devon's configured limits (or is confirmed by Devon after escalation), it completes. *(Whether the Agentic Token used is scoped to that single transaction is not stated in the sources this case study relies on, and this illustration does not assume it.)*

**Step 8b — Escalation to Devon.** Two distinct conditions route the transaction back to Devon rather than completing automatically: it falls outside a spending limit or timeframe Devon actively set (Step 5); or it sits in a category Devon never configured and the Gate's decision does not approve it (Step 6). Malformed input, an unreadable transaction date, and registration or ownership failures are not escalations: they are rejected outright, before Devon is asked anything (Steps 2–4). This illustration does not merge these into one generic "blocked" outcome, since each reflects a meaningfully different finding about the transaction or about the state of Devon's account.

**What this illustration does not include:** a description of how DI Pro's fraud scoring, if at all, applies to an agent-initiated transaction specifically — no Mastercard source connects DI Pro's scoring pipeline to Agent Pay's authorization flow, and this case study does not construct that connection; any specific default spending limit or permission state; and any claim about what share of agent-initiated transactions actually resolve without returning to the consumer, since Mastercard has not disclosed a resolution or automation rate for Agent Pay comparable to Lemonade's 96%/55% figures for AI Jim.

## 4b. Reference Implementation

A working reference implementation accompanies this case study — grounding the confirmed functions described in Section 3.2 and the illustrated workflow in Section 4, built and tested directly within this drafting session. Like the Lloyds entry earlier in this series, this is a procedural departure from the CommBank-through-Zurich pattern of reconciling an independent collaborator's build against an already-drafted Section 4: there was no second party and no separate submission to reconcile here. Section 4's workflow and this implementation were developed against each other in the same pass, and — a further departure worth naming, since no prior entry in this series has done this — the build then went through two separate, sequential rounds of scrutiny before being finalized (and, later, a third, described below): a deliberate adversarial testing pass, and a subsequent review pass aimed specifically at what the adversarial pass wasn't looking for. Both found real problems. This case study treats that as a strength of this entry's process, not a mark against it — a single clean test run, even a large one, is evidence about the tests that were run, not a general certificate of correctness, and this entry's own history is the clearest demonstration of that principle anywhere in the series so far.

It runs entirely on fabricated mock data, with no external services, credentials, or Mastercard systems involved anywhere in the repository.

**Why Agent Pay, and not Decision Intelligence Pro.** DI Pro's confirmed record (Section 3.1) is almost entirely outcome figures — modelling percentages, a false-positive reduction claim — with essentially no disclosed mechanism beyond "assessing the relationships between multiple entities surrounding a transaction." There is no comparably-shaped confirmed *process* to build a pipeline against. Agent Pay's confirmed record is the opposite shape: several distinct, sequential functions (registration, verification, consumer-configured permission dimensions, human referral, an intent record) that describe a process, not just a result.

**Architecture.** A single, linear pipeline — **Input Validation → Registration/Verification → Agent-Consumer Ownership Check → Permission/Limit Check → Authorization Gate (ambiguous cases only) → Verifiable Intent Record** — run by an orchestrator in strict, fail-fast sequence, not a multi-agent system. The Gate is deliberately narrow in scope: invoked only when a transaction falls in a category the consumer never configured one way or the other. An amount exceeding an actively-set limit, or a date outside an actively-set window, is treated as an unambiguous violation of something the consumer explicitly configured — nothing to decide, only something to compare — and escalates directly without the Gate ever running. This split is this case study's own architectural choice, not something Mastercard's disclosure distinguishes one way or the other, and is stated as such rather than implied.

**What's Confirmed:** agents must be registered and verified before they can transact; Agentic Tokens are configurable — restrictable by agent, merchant, category, spending limit, timeframe, or usage rule; Verifiable Intent creates a cryptographically verifiable record of what a consumer authorized.

**What's Constructed:** the mock registered-agent directory; the specific permission schema (category, spending limit, timeframe — merchant-level and usage-rule-level restriction are accepted as fields but not themselves checked, a named scope limit); the registration/verification check as a plain boolean lookup rather than a real Web Bot Auth signature validation; the Verifiable Intent record as a plain Python object rather than a cryptographically signed artifact; and the agent-consumer ownership check itself, whose absence in this repository's first version was a genuine correctness gap caught during review, not an undisclosed mechanism left unmodeled by choice.

**What's deliberately absent — the Authorization Gate.** Ships with zero default authorization criteria — no spending-limit cutoff, no category allowlist, no confidence score, under any label. Raises an error at construction if no external decision function is supplied, and strictly validates whatever that function returns.

**Test coverage.** **82 tests across 9 files, 82 passing, 0 failing**, verified in a freshly created, empty Python virtual environment against a cache-free copy of the repository after each review round (72 across 8 files before the third round). Several tests use mock/spy assertions to prove sequencing directly — confirming the Gate is invoked only for genuinely unconfigured categories and never for an unambiguous limit or timeframe violation.

**What building it surfaced — separate rounds, not one.** A first, deliberate adversarial testing pass — attacking the pipeline with wrong-typed values, `NaN`, negative amounts, and a string in place of a date — found four problems: a negative amount and a `NaN` amount both completed silently, as if properly checked and found acceptable; a string-typed amount or date crashed the pipeline outright; a missing category was treated as an ordinary unconfigured one rather than as invalid input. All four were fixed by an input-validation stage ahead of registration.

A second, separate review pass — looking specifically for gaps the adversarial pass wasn't aimed at — found one problem serious enough to call a genuine correctness defect: **the pipeline never verified that an agent actually belonged to the consumer it claimed to transact for.** Confirmed directly against the running pipeline before the fix: an agent registered to one consumer, claiming to act for a different, unrelated consumer, completed a transaction and generated a Verifiable Intent record falsely attributing authorization to that unrelated party. The same pass also found that `datetime.datetime` — a subclass of `datetime.date` in Python — silently passed the original date-validation check and then crashed downstream; that a transaction violating both the spending limit and the active timeframe simultaneously had a real but untested and undocumented deterministic outcome; and several smaller documentation issues. All are fixed and covered by regression tests.

A third review pass asked one question of every branch: what else could arrive here looking exactly like this? The first two rounds' most serious findings were all answers to it (a `NaN` or negative amount passing as within-limit; a stranger's account and a missing category passing as a new category). Asked once more, it found that the permission lookup matched category labels exactly, so a configured category written differently — `Household_Staples` for Devon's `household_staples` — came back as unconfigured and went to the Gate. Confirmed against the running pipeline before the fix: Devon's groceries, dated after Devon's active window, escalated as `household_staples` and completed as `Household_Staples`, bypassing a restriction Devon had actively set. Category labels are now compared in one canonical spelling, and a label made only of separators is rejected as malformed. Synonyms are deliberately not mapped: `groceries` is still treated as a category Devon never configured, because deciding which labels mean the same thing would mean inventing a category taxonomy Mastercard has not disclosed. The category therefore remains a label the agent asserts, not one the pipeline verifies — stated here as a limitation, not resolved by inference.

**Known limitations:** no real cryptography anywhere; mock data covers three registered agents across two consumers, not Mastercard's actual scale; no connection is modeled between DI Pro's fraud scoring and Agent Pay's authorization flow; the category is a label the agent asserts, not one the pipeline verifies (spelling variants are normalised; synonyms are not mapped); and no review round was exhaustive — the first two found and closed eleven real issues and the third two more, which demonstrates that looking for problems mattered, three times, not that no further defects remain.

**Explicit non-claims.** This repository is not a disclosure of Mastercard's actual Agent Pay system. It is built from what Mastercard has publicly confirmed, plus explicitly labeled construction everywhere the public record stops, with the Authorization Gate's total absence of default criteria standing as the clearest expression of where that record actually ends.

## 5. Documented Results

This section separates cleanly into three kinds of figures — Decision Intelligence Pro's pre-launch modelling, Agent Pay's confirmed reach without any confirmed performance, and the general (not product-specific) industry survey data Mastercard has separately commissioned — kept apart rather than blended into a single "Mastercard's AI results" narrative, consistent with this series' practice at CommBank of not treating a company's separate disclosures as one continuous story simply because they touch the same broad theme.

### 5.1 Decision Intelligence Pro: Pre-Launch Figures, Never Updated

At its February 1, 2024 announcement, Mastercard gave three distinct figures for DI Pro, and this case study keeps them as three, not one: initial modelling showing average fraud-detection improvements of 20% (up to 300% in some instances); a separate claim, attributed to then-president of Cyber and Intelligence Ajay Bhalla's description of "our own analysis," that the solution reduces false positives by more than 85%; and a stated intent for the technology to scan "an unprecedented one trillion data points" and improve DI's score in under 50 milliseconds once available. All three are 2024, pre-availability figures — the release itself states DI Pro "will be available from later this year," so none of them describe measured, in-production performance even at the time they were given. No source reviewed for this case study — not Mastercard's newsroom, its subsequent 10-K or 10-Q filings, its investor presentations, or its 2025–2026 earnings-call transcripts — restates, updates, or reports an actual post-launch performance figure for DI Pro specifically. This case study does not treat the 2024 figures as current, and does not construct an updated one in their place.

### 5.2 Agent Pay: Reach Confirmed, Performance Not

Agent Pay's documented results are entirely about where the program has rolled out, not what it has achieved once live — the same asymmetry this series found at Lloyds for its customer-facing financial assistant. The confirmed rollout sequence: Citi and U.S. Bank Mastercard cardholders first (per the September 10, 2025 release, ahead of "all U.S. Mastercard cardholders" being enabled "before the holiday season"); on the Q4 2025 earnings call (January 29, 2026), Miebach stated U.S. issuers had been enabled and the company was "working to enable our global issuer base by the end of the first quarter" of 2026; regional expansions followed into Latin America and the Caribbean (announced December 2025) and Australia, where Mastercard stated its "first authenticated agentic transactions" occurred January 28, 2026. Secondary reporting on Mastercard's Q1 2026 earnings call describes issuer enablement as having reached "nearly all" of Mastercard's global issuer base by that point — this case study attributes that specific phrasing to the secondary summary rather than to a verbatim Mastercard quote, since it was not found stated in those exact terms in a primary Mastercard release.

Beyond reach, no source reviewed discloses a transaction volume, a resolution rate, an escalation-to-consumer rate, or any accuracy or satisfaction figure for Agent Pay at any stage. The only executive commentary on the program's real-world state is qualitative: Miebach's Q4 2025 remark that agentic commerce "represents another avenue to enable payment choice with the same trust we always deliver... It's early days, but we are ready," and his later, similarly qualitative Q2 2026 characterization of engagement as "early days" but "energizing" across global markets. Neither is a metric. This case study does not manufacture a resolution rate or adoption figure to sit alongside Agent Pay's rollout milestones, consistent with the standard this series applied to Zurich's Clara and Lloyds' financial assistant.

### 5.3 The FT Longitude Survey: Industry-Wide Results, Not Attributed to a Specific Mastercard Product

Mastercard's June 2025 survey, commissioned from Financial Times Longitude and covering 300 senior fraud and risk executives across issuing banks, acquiring banks, payment facilitators, payment processors, merchants/retailers, and digital wallet providers, produced the richest quantified figures in this case study — and none of them are attributed to Decision Intelligence Pro, Agent Pay, or any other named Mastercard product specifically. The survey asked about AI use in fraud prevention generally: 42% of issuers and 26% of acquirers reported saving more than $5 million from fraud attempts over the prior two years with the help of AI; separately, 83% of all respondents said AI had significantly reduced false positives and customer churn rates over the prior 12 months — a different, shorter timeframe than the 42%/26% figure, and a distinct statistic from a second, separately reported 83% figure in the same survey describing reduced fraud-investigation time. This case study keeps all three figures separate and does not conflate any of them with one another, or with Decision Intelligence Pro's own launch-time figures in Section 5.1, since the survey question was about AI use broadly across respondents' own organizations, not about a Mastercard product specifically.

### 5.4 Consumer Readiness Figures: Sentiment, Not Measured Usage

Mastercard's August 2026 Signals materials add one further category of figure, already introduced in Section 3.4 for a different purpose: 74% of consumers surveyed said they were open to letting an agent complete specific commerce tasks at their request, while willingness to let an agent complete a purchase fully autonomously was reported as "one in ten," sourced by Mastercard to a Gartner survey (322 U.S. consumers, January 2026) that itself describes the figure as topping out at 11% in lower-stakes categories. These are readiness and attitude figures about AI agents generally, not measured usage data about Agent Pay specifically, and this case study does not present them as evidence of how Agent Pay is actually performing.

### 5.5 Reading These Results Together

No figure in this section describes the same thing twice, and none should be read as corroborating another. DI Pro's figures are frozen at 2024, pre-launch estimates. Agent Pay's figures describe geographic and issuer reach, not outcomes. The FT Longitude survey describes industry-wide AI use in fraud prevention, not a Mastercard product. The Signals/Gartner figures describe consumer sentiment toward AI agents in general, not Agent Pay usage. A reader could reasonably want a single "how well is Mastercard's agentic AI working" figure; the public record, as of this case study's writing, does not provide one for any of Mastercard's named AI systems, and this case study does not construct one on Mastercard's behalf.

## 6. Limitations, Failures, and Honest Caveats

### 6.1 DI Pro's Figures Are Frozen at Launch, With No Confirmed Update Since

As established in Section 5.1, every quantified DI Pro figure this case study reports — the 20%/300% modelling range, the 85%+ false-positive reduction, the one-trillion-data-point and sub-50-millisecond claims — dates to the February 1, 2024 announcement and is explicitly forward-looking or pre-availability language even at the time it was given. More than two years on from that announcement, no Mastercard disclosure reviewed for this case study restates, updates, or supersedes any of these figures with an actual measured, post-launch result. This is a materially thinner and staler outcome record than this series found for comparably-aged systems elsewhere — Klarna's AI assistant, for instance, moved from a February 2024 launch figure to a dated, updated 2025 figure the company itself provided. DI Pro has no analogous second data point. This case study treats that absence as a finding in its own right, not as license to assume the original figures still hold.

### 6.2 A Genuine Architecture Inconsistency in How DI Pro's Model Has Been Described

Section 3.1 already states this, and it is worth naming again here as a limitation of the sourcing available, not just a passing caveat: CNBC's February 2024 coverage describes DI Pro's underlying model as a "proprietary recurrent neural network," while quoting Bhalla, in the same article, describing the technology as using "transformer models." Mastercard's own press release uses neither term. This case study does not resolve this inconsistency in either direction — it is not this case study's place to decide which secondary description is more accurate when Mastercard's own primary language simply doesn't specify — and states plainly that no reviewed source reconciles the two.

### 6.3 The Central Finding of This Entry: Two Genuinely Separate Initiatives, at Risk of Being Read as One Continuous Story

As Section 2 states directly, DI Pro (a fraud-scoring enhancement, announced February 2024) and Agent Pay (agentic-commerce payment infrastructure, announced April 2025) address two different operational problems, disclosed fourteen months apart, with no primary source connecting them technically or narratively. Nothing in Mastercard's own record states that agentic commerce created or worsened the fraud problem DI Pro addresses, or that DI Pro was built in anticipation of agentic transactions. This case study's own research also surfaced a specific secondary-coverage risk worth flagging directly: a third-party explainer on eco.com describes Decision Intelligence as scoring "agent-initiated" transactions with "new agentic-specific signals, including agent identity" — a claim no Mastercard source supports, and one that would, if repeated uncritically, fuse these two systems into exactly the kind of single dramatic narrative this series first documented at CommBank (an autonomous "Dispute Resolution Agent" that both handled disputes and caused layoffs — two real, separately-sourced systems reported as one). This case study does not repeat that fusion, and states directly that DI Pro's fraud-scoring pipeline and Agent Pay's authorization flow are not disclosed as connected anywhere in Mastercard's own materials.

### 6.4 An Unresolved Tension in Mastercard's Own Verifiable Intent Disclosure

As Section 3.3 states directly and does not resolve: a secondary summary of Mastercard's Q1 2026 earnings call describes Verifiable Intent using "launched" language — not independently confirmed as Mastercard's own verbatim wording — in the same period the FIDO Alliance's own April 2026 materials describe the underlying multi-network standard as still under collaborative development within its Payments Technical Working Group. This case study cannot determine from the public record whether "launched" refers to production availability inside Agent Pay's intent APIs specifically, or to the earlier-stated release of a specification and reference implementation. Both readings are available; neither is confirmed. This case study states both accounts side by side rather than picking one, consistent with this series' practice of preserving a genuine ambiguity rather than smoothing it into a cleaner-sounding sentence.

### 6.5 Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol Is Easily, and Wrongly, Read as a Competing Standard

As Section 3.2 establishes, Mastercard's Agent Pay Acceptance Framework and Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol were announced the same day (October 14, 2025), both built on Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth layer. Cloudflare's own account describes partnering with both networks in parallel, with each network hosting its own registered-agent directory on that shared authentication layer — a structurally different relationship than "competing standards," which would imply the two are mutually exclusive or incompatible approaches to the same problem. This case study states the parallel-implementation framing directly because a less careful reading — of two large card networks announcing rival-sounding programs on the same day — could easily default to "competing," and that framing is not what Cloudflare's own materials support.

### 6.6 Web Bot Auth's Standards Status Has Changed Since Launch, and This Case Study Dates That Change Precisely

At the Agent Pay Acceptance Framework's October 2025 launch, Web Bot Auth existed only as individual IETF Internet-Drafts — an early-stage proposal, not an adopted standard. As of this case study's writing, the IETF's webbotauth working group has adopted a formal working-group draft (dated September 2026), which is a real step toward standardization but still carries no confirmed RFC status. This case study treats "built on an emerging, not-yet-finalized standard" as accurate throughout, and does not upgrade Web Bot Auth's status based on the September 2026 development without also stating plainly that it remains short of a finalized RFC.

### 6.7 The Gartner "One in Ten" Figure Is a Narrower Claim Than Mastercard's Own Framing Suggests

As Section 3.4 establishes, Mastercard's August 2026 Signals materials cite Gartner for the claim that "only one in ten" consumers are willing to let an agent complete a purchase autonomously. Gartner's own release states the underlying figure more narrowly: willingness "topped out at 11%" specifically in lower-stakes categories such as personal care and household supplies, from a survey of 322 U.S. consumers. Mastercard's own release restates this as a general "one in ten" without repeating the category-specific or geographic scope. This case study states the narrower, more precisely scoped Gartner framing directly, rather than repeating Mastercard's broader restatement of its own source without qualification.

### 6.8 No System-Specific Automation or Resolution Rate Exists for Agent Pay

Unlike Lemonade's AI Jim (96%/55% automation figures) or even Lloyds' AI HR Assistant (a single disclosed first-contact resolution rate), Mastercard has disclosed no comparable figure for Agent Pay — no share of agent-initiated transactions that complete without returning to the consumer, no confirmed volume of transactions processed through the Acceptance Framework, no accuracy or dispute rate for Verifiable Intent records. This is not a gap this case study can close by inference from adjacent figures; Section 5.2 already states this plainly, and this section reiterates it as a limitation of the sourcing record itself, not an oversight in this case study's research.

### 6.9 Self-Reported, Unaudited Figures Throughout

Every quantitative figure in this case study — the DI Pro modelling percentages, the FT Longitude survey results, the Signals/Gartner consumer-readiness figures, and Mastercard's own firm-level financial figures in Section 1 — originates in Mastercard's own disclosures, a survey Mastercard itself commissioned, or (for the Gartner figure specifically) a third party Mastercard chose to cite. None has been independently audited or re-verified by a party outside this case study's own research process. This case study presents these as Mastercard's own numbers and Mastercard's own choice of which third-party figures to cite, not as independently confirmed facts about the real-world accuracy, adoption, or security performance of any system named in this entry.

## 7. Forward-Looking: Mastercard in the Agentic Era

> **Editorial analysis.** This section draws on publicly stated positions and should be read as informed projection, not documented fact.

Mastercard's own public statements through 2026 point toward accelerating, not slowing, investment in agentic commerce specifically. What launched in April 2025 as a single named program has, in the space of roughly eighteen months, spawned a family of related but distinct initiatives: the Agent Pay Acceptance Framework (October 2025), Verifiable Intent with Google (March 2026), Mastercard Agent Suite for businesses building their own agents (March 2026), Agent Pay for Machines — a stated "complementary" service for machine-to-machine transactions (June 2026) — and Agent Connect for merchants (September 2026). Mastercard's own language keeps describing this as early-stage even as the list of shipped components grows: Miebach's "it's early days, but we are ready" (Q4 2025) and "early days, but energizing" (Q2 2026) bracket a period in which the company visibly kept building rather than pausing to consolidate. Whether that pace of feature releases is itself evidence of confidence, or evidence of a company still finding the shape of the problem, is not something the public record settles either way.

The competitive and standards backdrop is moving in the same window. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol, Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth progressing from individual Internet-Drafts toward a working-group document, and the FIDO Alliance's own standards effort incorporating Mastercard's Verifiable Intent contribution all suggest an industry still converging on shared infrastructure rather than one already settled. Mastercard sits inside that convergence rather than above it — its own framework depends on the same Web Bot Auth layer Visa uses, and its own Verifiable Intent contribution is subject to the same multi-party standards process Visa's team also sits on. Whether Mastercard's specific implementations end up matching whatever the industry eventually standardizes on, or require rework once Web Bot Auth and the FIDO agent-interaction standards actually finalize, is not indicated anywhere in the public record as of this case study's writing.

Three things this case study cannot resolve remain genuinely open, and are named here rather than guessed at. First, whether Decision Intelligence Pro's frozen 2024 figures will ever be updated with a real, post-launch performance number — more than two years without one is itself a data point, but it does not tell a reader whether an update is coming or whether Mastercard has simply moved on to newer disclosures elsewhere in its fraud-and-security portfolio. Second, whether Mastercard will ever disclose a resolution or automation rate for Agent Pay comparable to what Lemonade discloses for AI Jim — the reach-without-performance pattern documented in Section 5.2 could close as the program matures, or could persist indefinitely if Mastercard's disclosure habit continues to favor rollout milestones over outcome metrics. Third, whether the Verifiable Intent "launched" tension identified in Section 3.3 and Section 6.4 will resolve toward confirmed production integration or remain an open question the next entry in this series would need to revisit.

The more durable observation, and the one this case study's Section 6 findings support most directly, is not any single one of these open questions but the shape they share. This entry documented two systems disclosed fourteen months apart that address genuinely different problems and risk being read as one continuous "AI created the problem AI now solves" story if a reader isn't careful; and, separately, a newer system whose rollout Mastercard has been eager to narrate in geographic and issuer-enablement terms while saying almost nothing about how well it actually works once live. Those are two different findings, not one, but they point in the same direction: Mastercard's public disclosure of its agentic-commerce build-out has, so far, been considerably more forthcoming about *where* it is happening than about *what happens* once it does. Whether that changes as Agent Pay, Agent Pay for Machines, and Verifiable Intent all move further from launch and closer to routine operation is not something this case study can predict. It is the question a reader tracking this space should carry forward.
